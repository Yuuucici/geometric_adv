Abstract
3D vision-language models (3D VLMs) are moving beyond
object-level point-cloud recognition toward scene-level ques-
tion answering, natural-language grounding, relation-aware
captioning, and embodied decision making. This shift creates
a security problem that is not captured by classical 3D adver-
sarial benchmarks: a model may correctly recognize all rele-
vant objects while giving an incorrect answer about their spa-
tial relation. Such relation-level failures are subtle, because the
semantic layer appears intact, yet safety-critical decisions such
as target selection, manipulation, and navigation can be cor-
rupted. We introduce RelAttack3D, a query-conditioned rela-
tional adversarial attack for point-cloud VLMs. Given a point-
cloud scene and a natural-language query, RelAttack3Dparses
the query into a relation graph, localizes the involved objects,
mines relation-critical points, and optimizes constrained per-
turbations that induce relation-level errors while preserving
object semantics, local geometry, point density, and sensor
plausibility.
Unlike classification-critical point attacks, Re-
lAttack3Dexplicitly models relation-dependent geometric ev-
idence, including centroid projections, support/contact sur-
faces, nearest inter-object point pairs, containment bound-
aries, and free-space corridor margins. We further propose
RelBench-3D, a relation-robustness evaluation protocol span-
ning 3D visual question answering, natural-language ground-
ing, relation-aware captioning, and embodied affordance judg-
ment. Finally, we introduce Relation-Consistency Verification,
a lightweight detection baseline based on logical query rewrit-
ing, explicit geometric checking, and viewpoint consistency.
This manuscript provides a detailed top-conference-style for-
mulation, method, benchmark construction procedure, imple-
mentation plan, ablation design, and result-table templates.
Numerical entries are intentionally left as placeholders and
must be replaced by real experiments before submission.

Introduction
3D vision-language models (3D VLMs) are becoming a nat-
ural interface between geometric perception and language-
driven reasoning. Early point-cloud learning focused on shape
classification, part segmentation, semantic segmentation, ob-
ject detection, registration, completion, and reconstruction.
Recent 3D VLMs instead ask a more ambitious question: can
a model use point clouds as evidence for open-ended lan-
guage interaction? PointLLM connects point-cloud encoders
with large language models for object-level point-language in-
struction following [5]; GPT4Point presents a unified point-
language framework for understanding and generation [6]; and
3D-LLM injects 3D world representations into large language
models to support 3D question answering, dense captioning,
grounding, dialogue, and navigation [7]. These models sug-
gest that point clouds are no longer merely inputs to discrimi-
native geometric networks. They increasingly serve as multi-
modal evidence for language-conditioned spatial reasoning.
This change forces a change in how robustness should be
defined. In classical point-cloud adversarial learning, the dom-
inant attack goal is object-level misclassification: a perturbed
airplane is classified as a chair, or a perturbed chair is classi-
fied as a table. This threat model remains relevant, but it is
incomplete for 3D VLMs. A 3D VLM may correctly recog-
nize a chair and a table, produce a fluent description, and still
answer incorrectly when asked whether the chair is to the left
of the table. It may recognize a sofa and a wall, yet incorrectly
conclude that a robot can pass between them. It may identify a
cup and a desk, but fail to judge whether the cup is on the desk
or under it. These failures are not merely semantic recognition
errors; they are relation-level reasoning failures.
Relation-level failures are especially dangerous because
spatial relations are the bridge between perception and action.
Directional relations such as left/right and front/behind sup-
port target search and navigation. Support relations such as
on/under support manipulation. Distance and comparative re-
lations support object disambiguation. Topological and affor-
dance relations such as between, blocked, passable, reachable,
and inside support embodied decision making. A system that
preserves object recognition but corrupts these relations may
appear reliable to a human evaluator while still causing down-
stream planning or interaction failures.
Existing 3D adversarial attacks have shown that point-cloud
classifiers and perception systems are vulnerable to point per-
turbation, point addition, point dropping, and minimal adver-
sarial modifications [12, 13, 14]. Emerging work has also be-
gun to study the adversarial robustness of 3D large VLMs and
shows that point-based 3D VLMs can be vulnerable to vision-
token and caption-level attacks [16]. However, these studies
do not directly address a key question:
1

===== Page 2 =====
Can an adversary preserve the semantic identity of
the relevant objects and the plausibility of the point
cloud while selectively hijacking the spatial relation
reasoning of a 3D VLM under a natural-language
query?
We argue that this question defines a distinct and underex-
plored attack surface. A successful relational attack should not
simply make the model confused about what objects exist. It
should keep the chair as a chair, the table as a table, and the
scene visually plausible, while flipping or corrupting the an-
swer to a relation query. This is a stronger and more diagnostic
criterion than generic caption degradation.
Key observation.
The points that determine object identity
are not necessarily the points that determine a spatial relation.
For recognizing a chair, the chair back, seat, armrests, and legs
may be discriminative. For answering whether the chair is left
of a table, however, relation-relevant evidence may be con-
centrated around projected centroids, lateral boundaries, near-
est inter-object surfaces, or viewpoint-dependent bounding-
box order. For answering whether a robot can pass between
a sofa and a wall, the relevant points are not the most class-
discriminative sofa points, but the clearance boundary and
free-space corridor margin. Thus, attacking a 3D VLM’s rela-
tion reasoning requires a query-conditioned and relation-aware
perturbation strategy.
We propose RelAttack3D, a query-conditioned relational
adversarial attack framework for point-cloud VLMs. Given
a point cloud P and a natural-language query q, RelAt-
tack3Dfirst parses the query into a structured relation graph.
It then localizes the involved objects and computes a relation-
critical score for each point by combining model sensitivity,
relation-specific geometric support, boundary importance, and
reference-frame consistency.
Only the top-ranked relation-
critical points are perturbed. The perturbation is optimized
to induce a target relation error while preserving object se-
mantics, global geometry, local smoothness, point density, and
sensor plausibility.
The goal of this paper is not to claim a minor improvement
over existing point-cloud attacks. The goal is to establish a
more precise robustness problem for 3D VLMs: semantic-
preserving relation hijacking. If a small, plausible point-cloud
perturbation can systematically change relation answers while
leaving object recognition intact, then object-level robustness
and caption-level robustness are insufficient proxies for 3D
VLM safety.
Contributions.
This manuscript makes the following contri-
butions:
• We formulate semantic-preserving relational attack as a
new robustness problem for 3D VLMs. A valid attack must
corrupt a relation answer while preserving the identities of
the queried objects and satisfying geometric and sensor-
plausibility constraints.
• We introduce query-conditioned relation-critical point
mining,
which selects points according to language-
conditioned relation evidence rather than classification
saliency alone.
• We design relation-specific attack objectives for 3D VQA,
natural-language grounding, relation-aware captioning, and
embodied affordance judgment.
• We propose RelBench-3D, a benchmark protocol that ex-
tracts relation-centric samples from ScanQA, ScanRefer,
SQA3D, ReferIt3D-style referring expressions, and embod-

• We provide a lightweight defense baseline, Relation-
Consistency Verification, which checks logical equiva-

• We provide detailed implementation, evaluation, ablation,

Related Work
2.1
Point-Cloud Representation Learning
Deep learning on point clouds has progressed from sim-
ple permutation-invariant architectures to hierarchical, graph-
based, transformer-based, and masked-pretraining models.
PointNet [1] introduced a symmetric aggregation mechanism
for unordered point sets. PointNet++ [2] added hierarchical
local feature learning. DGCNN [3] used dynamic graphs to
capture local geometric neighborhoods, while Point-BERT [4]
explored masked point modeling for 3D representation pre-
training. These models remain important as point encoders for
downstream 3D-language systems.
However, representation learning alone does not solve re-
lation reasoning.
A point encoder may recognize object
geometry, but relation reasoning requires object localiza-
tion, reference-frame interpretation, relational comparison,
and sometimes physical affordance reasoning. Our work stud-
ies failures at this relation layer.
2.2
3D Vision-Language Models
3D VLMs connect point-cloud perception with natural-
language understanding. PointLLM [5] aligns point-cloud fea-
tures with a large language model and supports generative
3D object classification and captioning. GPT4Point [6] pro-
poses a unified framework for point-language understanding
and generation. 3D-LLM [7] extends large language models
into 3D scenes and supports 3D captioning, question answer-
ing, grounding, task decomposition, and navigation.
These systems demonstrate a transition from object-level
point-language alignment to scene-level reasoning.
As 3D
VLMs are increasingly used for grounded interaction, robust-
ness must be evaluated not only by whether objects are named

correctly, but also by whether spatial relations and action-
relevant properties remain reliable.
2.3
3D
Spatial
Question
Answering
and

ScanRefer [8] introduced natural-language 3D object local-
ization in RGB-D scans. ScanQA [9] formulated 3D ques-
tion answering for spatial scene understanding. SQA3D [10]
considered situated question answering, where the agent’s
position and orientation affect the answer.
ReferIt3D-style
datasets [11] contain fine-grained referring expressions that of-
ten depend on relational object disambiguation.
These benchmarks are useful foundations for relational ro-
bustness, but they were not designed as adversarial robustness
benchmarks. They typically evaluate clean-input task accuracy
rather than whether relation answers are stable under small,
structured, query-conditioned point-cloud perturbations.
2.4
Adversarial Attacks on 3D Point Clouds
Xiang et al. [12] introduced adversarial point-cloud genera-
tion and perturbation attacks.
Later work studied minimal
adversarial point modifications [13], transferability, outlier-
aware constraints, robust training, and defense. Surveys sum-
marize attack types such as point shifting, point dropping,
point adding, transformation attacks, and optimization-based
attacks [14].
Most of these attacks target classifiers or perception mod-
ules. Their success criterion is usually a wrong class label
or wrong detection result. By contrast, RelAttack3Dtargets
language-conditioned relation answers while explicitly pre-
serving the semantic identities of the queried objects. This
distinction matters because an attack that changes “chair” into
“table” does not prove that relation reasoning is vulnerable; it
only proves that object recognition is vulnerable.
2.5
Adversarial
Robustness
of
Vision-
Language Models
Large VLMs are known to be vulnerable to adversarial visual
perturbations, and robustness evaluation has become an im-
portant topic in 2D multimodal learning [15]. Recent work
on 3D large VLM robustness studies adversarial vulnerabil-
ity in point-based 3D VLMs and introduces vision attacks and
caption attacks [16]. This confirms that adversarial evaluation
should be extended to 3D multimodal models.
Our work differs by focusing on a structured failure mode:
relation hijacking. Instead of simply degrading captions or
forcing arbitrary target text, we ask whether a model can be
made wrong about a specific relation while still recognizing
the involved objects. This makes the attack more diagnostic
for embodied and safety-critical settings.

3.2
Reference Frames
Directional relations are undefined without a reference frame.
The phrase “left of the table” may refer to a world frame, a
camera frame, an observer frame, a robot ego frame, or an
object-centric frame. We therefore define a directional relation
as
r = (rtype, π),
(6)
where π specifies the coordinate convention or pose.
In
datasets without reliable view or agent pose, we do not eval-
uate left/right and front/behind as primary relation labels. In-
stead, we focus on viewpoint-invariant or weakly viewpoint-
dependent relations such as on/under, near/far, inside/outside,
between, adjacent to, and supported by. In situated datasets,
directional labels are evaluated under the provided pose de-
scription.
This design prevents a common benchmark flaw: measuring
attack success on a relation whose ground truth is ambiguous.
A top-conference submission must be explicit about this issue;
otherwise the attack may be rejected as ill-posed.

3.4
Valid Relational Attack
A conventional point-cloud attack is successful when the pre-
dicted class changes. A relational attack requires a stricter cri-
terion. Given a clean scene P, query q, clean answer y, target
wrong answer ytar, and adversarial scene Padv, a sample is a
valid relational attack if it satisfies four conditions:
Relation error:
g(Fθ(Padv, q)) = ytar
or
g(Fθ(Padv, q))̸ = g(Fθ(P, q)),
(7)
Semantic preservation:
h(o | Padv) = h(o | P),
∀o ∈Oq,
(8)
Geometric plausibility:
Dgeo(P, Padv) ≤εg,
(9)
Sensor plausibility:
Padv ∈Ssensor.
(10)
where g maps a free-form answer to a normalized relation la-
bel, h is an object-recognition or object-grounding validator,
Oq is the set of objects involved in the query, Dgeo is a geo-
metric distance, and Ssensor is a set of sensor-plausible point
clouds.
The semantic preservation condition is crucial. Without it,
an attack can trivially flip a relation answer by destroying ob-
ject identity. Such a failure is not evidence of relation vulner-
ability.
3.5
Threat Models
We consider three threat models.
White-box attack. The attacker knows the model architec-
ture, parameters, and differentiable output distribution. This
setting measures worst-case vulnerability.
Transfer attack. The attacker has access to a surrogate
model Fϕ and transfers adversarial point clouds to a target
model Fθ. This setting is realistic because many deployed
VLMs are inaccessible but architecturally similar.
Query-only black-box attack. The attacker can only query
the target model and observe outputs. This setting uses score
estimation or discrete success feedback. We treat this as the
most deployment-relevant setting, but also the most expensive.

Method
4.1
Overview
Figure 1 gives the conceptual pipeline. RelAttack3Dtakes a
point cloud and a natural-language query as input. It parses
the query into relation tuples, localizes the relevant objects,
computes relation-critical point scores, and optimizes pertur-
bations only on selected points. The optimization objective
combines a relation attack loss with semantic, geometric, den-
sity, and sensor-plausibility regularizers.

A and B”.
• A language-model parser handles compositional or para-
phrased queries and returns a structured JSON-like repre-

4.3
Object Localization
For each object mention o ∈Oq, we obtain an object region
Ωo ⊂P. Depending on dataset and model access, Ωo may
come from one of the following:
• ground-truth instance masks or 3D bounding boxes;
• a frozen 3D detector or instance segmentor;
• the target 3D VLM’s grounding output;
• a combination of detector proposals and language matching.
In the main benchmark, we recommend reporting two set-
tings:
• Oracle localization: ground-truth object masks or boxes are
used for relation-critical point mining. This isolates relation
reasoning vulnerability from localization errors.
• Predicted localization: object proposals are produced by
an external detector or the target model. This measures end-
to-end vulnerability.
This separation is important. If the attack succeeds only
because object localization fails, the claim is weaker.
If it
succeeds even under oracle localization, the relation reason-
ing vulnerability is more convincing.

4.8
Complexity
Let N be the number of points, k the number of relation-
critical points, and T the number of optimization steps. Full-
point PGD requires gradients over all N points.
RelAt-
tack3Dreduces the optimization support to k ≪N, lower-
ing memory and improving interpretability.
The additional
cost comes from object localization and relation-critical scor-
ing. When ground-truth masks are available, relation scoring
is O(N) plus nearest-neighbor computations for selected re-
lation families. For distance and passability relations, nearest
point pairs and corridor boundaries can be computed approxi-
mately with voxel grids or k-d trees.

RelBench-3D: Benchmark Construc-
tion
A strong paper cannot rely on hand-picked examples.
We
therefore define RelBench-3D, a relation robustness protocol
constructed from existing 3D-language datasets.
5.1
Source Datasets
We recommend using the following sources:
• ScanQA: relation-rich 3D question answering over indoor
scenes.
• ScanRefer: natural-language 3D grounding with object ref-
erences.
• SQA3D: situated question answering with agent pose and
viewpoint information.
• ReferIt3D / Nr3D / Sr3D-style data: fine-grained referring
expressions with object disambiguation.
• Embodied simulator subset:
optional passability and
reachability questions built in Habitat, AI2-THOR, or an
equivalent simulator.
5.2
Sample Filtering
Each candidate sample must pass four filters:
1. The query contains a supported relation from Table 1.
2. The involved objects can be localized by ground truth or a
reliable detector.
3. The clean model answer is correct under the answer nor-
malizer.
4. The target wrong answer is valid and non-trivial.
The third filter is important. If the clean model already an-
swers incorrectly, the sample cannot measure adversarial vul-
nerability. It should be excluded from attack success statistics
or counted separately as clean failure.
7

===== Page 8 =====
5.3
Target Answer Construction
For binary questions, the target answer is often the opposite
label. For multi-choice or open-ended questions, the target an-
swer must be semantically plausible. For grounding, the target
box should be a distractor object that satisfies the same cat-
egory when possible but violates the target relation. For em-
bodied passability, the target action should be a safety-relevant
opposite label such as passable versus blocked.
5.4
Answer Normalization
Free-form VLM outputs require robust normalization. We use
a two-stage normalizer:
• A rule-based mapper handles yes/no, left/right, on/under,
and common synonyms.
• A frozen language judge maps longer answers to relation
labels, but only when confidence exceeds a threshold.
All ambiguous outputs are marked as invalid or separately
reported. This avoids inflating attack success by exploiting
answer-format noise.
5.5
Benchmark Splits
We recommend reporting results by relation family rather than
only aggregate scores. A model may be robust to on/under
but vulnerable to left/right. Aggregate attack success can hide
these differences.

Verification
Defense
A paper that only attacks may be viewed as incomplete. We
therefore propose Relation-Consistency Verification (RCV), a
lightweight defense and diagnostic baseline. RCV does not
claim to solve adversarial robustness, but it exposes relation
instability.
6.1
Logical Query Rewriting
For each query q, we generate logically equivalent or inverse-
consistent queries:
A left of B ⇔B right of A,
(39)
A on B ⇔B under A,
(40)
A closer to B than C ⇔A farther from C than B.
(41)
The language consistency score is

(42)
Low consistency indicates possible relation instability.
6.2
Explicit Geometric Checking
When object boxes or masks are available, we compute a geo-
metric relation label:
rgeo = ψ(Bs, Bt, π),
(43)
where ψ is a deterministic geometric checker. For example,
for left/right under frame π,
ψ(Bs, Bt, π) = sign(u⊤

(44)
A mismatch between rgeo and the VLM output rvlm is treated
as a warning.
6.3
Viewpoint Transformation Consistency
For viewpoint-dependent relations, we apply a known trans-
formation T to the point cloud and transform the query ac-
cordingly:
F(TP, Tq) ≈TF(P, q).
(45)
An adversarial sample may break this equivariance even when
the clean sample is stable.

We combine the three checks into a detection score:
DRCV = w1(1−Clang)+w2I[rgeo̸ = rvlm]+w3(1−Cview).
(46)
A sample is flagged if DRCV > τD.

7.2
Baselines
A convincing paper must compare against both generic attacks
and relation-specific ablations:
• Random noise: uniformly perturb selected points.
• Global PGD: optimize all points without relation-critical
mining.
• Classification-critical attack: select points using object
classification saliency.
• Point drop: remove relation or classification critical points.
• Point add: add points near object boundaries under density
constraints.
• Caption attack: optimize for a target caption without rela-
tion constraints.
• Geometry-only attack: use only relation geometry without
VLM gradients.
• RelAttack3D: full query-conditioned relation-critical at-
tack.
7.3
Metrics
We report the following metrics:
• RASR: relation attack success rate.
• TASR: targeted relation attack success rate.
• VASR: valid attack success rate, requiring relation error and
semantic preservation.
• SPR: semantic preservation rate.
• CD/HD: Chamfer and Hausdorff distances.
• Density shift: voxel-wise density distribution change.
• Outlier ratio: fraction of points violating local density or
surface consistency.
• Grounding shift: 1 −IoU(Bclean, Badv).
• Decision risk: unsafe or wrong embodied action rate.
• Query count: number of target-model queries for black-box
attacks.
The most important metric is VASR, not raw RASR. Raw
RASR can be inflated by destroying object semantics; VASR
cannot.

9
Discussion
9.1
Why This Is Not Just Another Point-Cloud
Attack
The novelty of RelAttack3Dis not the use of PGD, Cham-
fer distance, or saliency.
Those components are standard.
The novelty lies in the attack target and the point-selection
criterion.
Existing point-cloud attacks usually optimize for
object-level misclassification or perception failure.
RelAt-
tack3Doptimizes for relation-level failure under semantic
preservation.
This changes what counts as success, what
points should be perturbed, what datasets should be used, and
what metrics should be reported.
9.2
Failure Cases
We expect several failure cases:
• If a relation is determined by large-scale scene layout, small
local perturbations may be insufficient.
• If the target model uses explicit geometric computation, re-
lation hijacking may be harder.
• If object localization is unstable, it may be difficult to sepa-
rate localization failure from relation reasoning failure.
• If the answer normalizer is noisy, attack success may be
overestimated or underestimated.
• If directional labels lack a valid reference frame, the bench-
mark becomes ill-posed.

9.3
Limitations
This work is primarily a digital robustness study unless phys-
ical sensor experiments are added. A physically valid attack
would need to demonstrate perturbations through RGB-D re-
construction noise, LiDAR artifacts, reflective surfaces, ad-
versarial geometry, or small physical attachments. We also
assume that object localization is available or can be esti-
mated. In fully open-world scenes, object localization errors
may dominate relation reasoning errors.
Finally, many 3D
VLMs are not fully open source, so white-box claims must
be clearly separated from transfer and query-only claims.
9.4
Ethical Considerations
This work aims to identify and mitigate failures in 3D VLMs
before they are deployed in robotics, AR/VR, assistive naviga-
tion, and embodied agents. Experiments should be conducted
on public datasets and simulators. Released code should in-
clude evaluation scripts, defense baselines, and clear safety
documentation. The paper should avoid instructions for caus-
ing real-world sensor harm and should emphasize robustness
auditing.
10
Conclusion
We introduced RelAttack3D, a query-conditioned relational
adversarial attack framework for point-cloud vision-language
models. The main argument is that 3D VLM safety cannot
be reduced to object recognition robustness or caption robust-
ness. A model may preserve object semantics while failing at
the spatial relations that drive grounding, manipulation, and
navigation. RelAttack3Dexposes this vulnerability by parsing
language queries into relation graphs, mining relation-critical
points, and optimizing constrained perturbations that preserve
semantics and point-cloud plausibility. We further proposed
RelBench-3Dfor relation robustness evaluation and Relation-
Consistency Verification as a lightweight diagnostic defense.
The next step is empirical: implement the full pipeline, run ex-
periments across multiple 3D VLMs and relation-rich datasets,
and replace all placeholder results with reproducible measure-
ments.
